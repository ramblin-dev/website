# CTA placement research

Research notes on where to place calls-to-action on a landing page.

Source: Consensus search (queries on CTA placement / above-the-fold engagement / F-pattern reading). Citations link to the paper page on consensus.app.

## Findings

**Above the fold dominates attention, but the area below isn't dead.** Eye-tracking on e-commerce homepages found most fixations concentrate above the fold, yet users still scroll "unconsciously" and below-fold content does get viewed [1]. A separate homepage eye-tracking study confirmed above-the-fold content receives more fixations overall, with center-of-page fixations dominating during browsing and navigation-area fixations dominating during goal-seeking tasks [2]. Practical takeaway: the primary CTA must be visible without scrolling; a secondary CTA further down the page is worth it for scroll-through traffic.

**F-pattern reading is real for text-heavy pages, but not for visually structured ones.** The classic F-shape scan (top-left → across → down the left edge) holds for plain text pages, but breaks down when there's visual hierarchy — images, especially faces, redirect attention and pull fixations to otherwise-overlooked areas, even below the fold [2]. A three-column-vs-grid layout study found participants did *not* strictly follow F-shape on more structured layouts; column layouts in particular extended reading time and produced better information-finding [3]. Practical takeaway: on a minimal text page the eye lands top-left first, so the most important framing belongs there. A visually distinct element (a bordered form box, an image) can act as an "entry point" that breaks pure F-scan and pulls attention to the CTA.

**CTA findability on the first screen is the variable that moves conversion.** An A/B test on a university admissions site found the variant where the CTA was easy to locate on the first page converted at 68.5% vs. 49.1% for the variant where it wasn't [4]. A broader CRO survey of landing pages similarly identified design-component placement as a primary driver of conversion [5]. The Gutenberg-pattern paper from the human-factors literature argues landing pages benefit when the visual flow runs diagonally top-left to bottom-right, with the CTA landing in the "terminal area" of that scan [6].

## Synthesized rules of thumb

- **Primary CTA above the fold, every time.** Burying it below scroll measurably reduces conversion [4][5].
- **Give the CTA visual distinctness** — a framed box, contrasting background, or adjacent image — so it functions as an attention-pulling entry point rather than blending into the text scan [2][3].
- **Place the CTA where the natural scan ends, not where it begins.** For a top-left intro, that means the form sits just after the intro, near the Gutenberg "terminal area" [6].
- **Repeat the CTA at the bottom of long pages.** Scroll-through readers exist [1]; don't make them scroll back up to act.
- **Don't put competing links before the CTA.** Links peel attention away; surfacing them after the CTA preserves the chance to convert first.
